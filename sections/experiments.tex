\section{Experiments}

We evaluate UrbanDreamer in the SUMO traffic simulator \cite{lopez2018microscopic} on three urban road networks. The experiments consist of three parts: sample and computational efficiency, final routing quality, and the ablation study.

\subsection{Experimental Settings}

\subsubsection{Datasets and Road Networks}

We evaluate UrbanDreamer on three urban road networks from the RouteRL and URB benchmarks \cite{akman2025routerl,akman2026urb}: Gretz-Armainvilliers (4,485 road segments and 636 agents), Ingolstadt \cite{lobo2020intas} (853 road segments and 1,035 agents), and Fontenay-en-Parisis (7,585 road segments and 2,068 agents). We set the CAV penetration rate to 40\%. To create congested bottlenecks, we impose temporary speed reductions on selected SUMO edges in each original network. The reductions follow a fixed time schedule within every episode, are identical across episodes, and are shared by all compared methods, so they act as a stationary property of each benchmark rather than an exogenous stochastic event.


\subsubsection{Baselines}

We compare UrbanDreamer with two heuristic methods and four MARL methods. Free-flow greedy selects the candidate route with the minimum sum of free-flow edge travel times, whereas live-current greedy selects the route with the minimum sum of current edge travel-time estimates. The learning-based baselines include IQL \cite{iql}, IPPO \cite{ippo}, MAPPO \cite{mappo}, and MAMBA \cite{mamba}. The first three represent independent value learning, decentralized policy optimization, and centralized training with decentralized execution, respectively. MAMBA is a multi-agent model-based RL method that learns a latent world model in the Dreamer style, and therefore serves as the direct world-model baseline in our comparison. All methods share the same route-candidate interface, departure demand, and disruption schedule, are trained under the same simulation protocol until convergence, and are evaluated on identical episodes (implementation details in Appendix~\ref{app:baselines}); the environment-step counts reported below cover each method's complete training, including UrbanDreamer's warm-up data collection.

\subsubsection{Evaluation Metrics}

All methods are evaluated using the same route candidates, traffic demand, disruption settings, simulation horizon, and initial HDV state. The simulation horizon is set long enough for every vehicle to reach its destination before the episode ends. We use the global mean travel time \(T_{\mathrm{all}}\) as the primary metric and additionally report the CAV mean travel time \(T_{\mathrm{CAV}}\), the added cost \(C_{\mathrm{ALL}}\) relative to the pre-CAV reference state, and the 95th-percentile travel time \(T_{\mathrm{P95}}\).


\subsection{Efficiency and Scalability}

We evaluate UrbanDreamer in terms of sample efficiency, computational efficiency, and scalability. Specifically, we measure the number of environment steps required during training, the total training time, and how the computational cost changes with the road network scales.

\subsubsection{Sample Efficiency}

We measure sample efficiency by the number of real SUMO environment steps each method requires to converge. We declare convergence when the standard deviation of \(T_{\mathrm{all}}\) over a window of 20 consecutive training episodes falls below 0.06. For UrbanDreamer, this count includes the SUMO environment steps used for warm-up data collection.

Figure~\ref{fig:sample-efficiency-training-proxy} shows the training curves, and Table~\ref{tab:sample-efficiency} reports the environment steps required to reach convergence. UrbanDreamer requires 2,040, 2,220, and 2,160 environment steps on Gretz-Armainvilliers, Ingolstadt, and Fontenay-en-Parisis, respectively. Compared with MAPPO, which requires the fewest steps among the baselines, UrbanDreamer reduces the required environment steps by 42.4\%, 32.7\%, and 41.9\%.

Figure~\ref{fig:scalability_training_time} compares training time across networks with increasing numbers of road segments, from the 24-segment synthetic Toy pilot to Fontenay-en-Parisis with 7,585 segments. Training time increases for all methods as the network grows, while UrbanDreamer remains consistently faster than the baselines. Its training time increases from 5.566 hours on Ingolstadt (853 road segments) to 17.419 hours on Gretz-Armainvilliers (4,485 road segments) and 33.302 hours on Fontenay-en-Parisis (7,585 road segments), whereas the baselines require 78.2--96.1 hours on the largest network. Thus, UrbanDreamer's absolute training-time advantage becomes more pronounced as the road-network size increases.

\subsection{Ablation}

We evaluate the contributions of world model imagination, HDV response prediction, the vehicle and global critics, and the flow-matching transition model through controlled ablations. 

We conduct three groups of ablation experiments. Component ablation evaluates the effects of the main components on final routing quality, efficiency ablation isolates the contribution of world-model imagination to sample efficiency, and transition ablation compares the predictive quality of different transition models.


\subsubsection{Component Ablation}

We compare UrbanDreamer with four variants to assess the contribution of each component to routing performance. Actor-Critic removes world-model imagination, w/o HDV Response removes HDV influence prediction, Vehicle Critic Only retains only the per-vehicle critic, and MSE Transition replaces flow matching with deterministic next-state regression.



\input{table/ablation performance}

Table~\ref{tab:ablation performance} shows that removing any component degrades routing performance on all three networks. Removing world-model imagination (Actor-Critic) causes the largest degradation on Ingolstadt and Fontenay-en-Parisis, while removing the HDV response module is most harmful on Gretz-Armainvilliers. Replacing flow matching with MSE regression also degrades both routing performance and prediction quality (Table~\ref{tab:wm-prediction-ablation}).
