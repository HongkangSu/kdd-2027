\section{Introduction}

As connected and autonomous vehicles (CAVs) enter urban fleets, they share road networks with human-driven vehicles (HDVs), and such mixed traffic is becoming the default operating condition of urban road systems. The control problem it creates is mixed-autonomy dynamic routing: under time-varying origin--destination demand, a controller dynamically selects a route for each CAV while HDVs travel as an uncontrolled background, with the objective of minimizing the mean travel time over all vehicles \cite{calderone2017markov,lazar2020routing}.

What makes this problem difficult is the asymmetric coupling between the two populations. CAVs are directly controllable, whereas HDVs cannot be rerouted and can only be influenced indirectly: the congestion created by CAV route choices changes the travel costs that HDVs experience and hence their subsequent route choices. HDV responses in turn reshape the traffic state on which the next CAV decisions are made. Routing the CAV fleet is therefore not a one-shot assignment but a sequential decision problem in which the environment itself reacts to the policy.

Classical approaches formulate this interaction analytically: microscopic route choices are aggregated into macroscopic flows, and routes are assigned so that the network reaches an equilibrium under prescribed link-cost functions and behavioral assumptions \cite{tanaka2020linearly,cabannes2022solving,kolarich2022stackelberg}. These models are interpretable, but the aggregation discards microscopic state such as individual positions, departure times, and route candidates, and the behavioral side is hand-crafted: both the link costs and the HDV response to CAV-induced congestion must be fixed by rules before optimization starts, so every modeling simplification becomes an information loss in the resulting routing decisions.

Model-free multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) removes the prescribed equilibrium by letting CAVs learn state-dependent route policies directly in microscopic simulators \cite{lazar2021learning,shou2022multi,akman2025routerl,akman2026urb}. Two problems remain. First, the learned policy still acts against a rule-driven HDV background: human responses are generated by the simulator's built-in behavioral rules, so the environment dynamics that implicitly shape the policy are as hand-crafted as in analytical formulations. Second, model-free learning is expensive at urban scale: every policy update requires new simulator episodes, and the effect of a route choice on network-wide congestion materializes only after many decision intervals, so long-horizon credit assignment consumes a large number of costly environment interactions.

World-model reinforcement learning offers a direct answer to this cost: a predictive model of the environment transition is learned from collected trajectories, and the policy is optimized inside the learned model, so the bulk of policy updates consume imagined rather than real environment interactions \cite{hafnerdream,hafnermastering,hafner2025mastering}, a paradigm already validated in driving and multi-agent domains \cite{mamba,li2024think2drive,yang2026raw2drive}. Beyond this efficiency gain, the paradigm changes the modeling side of mixed-autonomy routing in two ways: the transition of the mixed HDV+CAV environment is estimated from data rather than specified by rules, replacing hand-crafted behavioral assumptions with a learned predictor; and because the learned transition is conditioned on CAV actions, it captures not only how HDVs behave but also how HDVs and CAVs influence each other through congestion, which is exactly the coupling that defines the problem.

Porting world models to mixed-autonomy routing is nevertheless not straightforward: the state is a graph-structured spatiotemporal traffic field, actions are vehicle-local route choices tied to a network-wide objective, and the uncontrolled HDV background adapts across episodes on a slower time scale than individual route decisions. A world model that jointly captures graph-level traffic transitions, HDV demand shifts, and vehicle-to-network credit assignment is still missing, and building one is the goal of this work.

To this end, we propose UrbanDreamer, a world-model-based MARL framework designed around these three challenges. A parameter-shared actor retains the executable control semantics of the routing problem, in which each active CAV selects a candidate route from its current segment to its destination. UrbanDreamer then projects the sampled route choices onto a planned CAV pressure field defined over road-segment nodes, placing vehicle actions and network dynamics in a common graph coordinate system. On top of this interface, a graph convolutional encoder represents the traffic field, and a state-to-state flow-matching model predicts its next latent representation conditioned on CAV pressure, departures, and a history-conditioned estimate of HDV route demand. During imagined rollouts, a deterministic vehicle wrapper preserves vehicle identities, route progress, departures, and arrivals, while centralized per-vehicle and global critics supply individual and network-level value signals. Simulator collection episodes periodically refresh the world model, and held-out simulator episodes serve as the basis for policy evaluation. Our contributions are summarized as follows.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=2pt,topsep=3pt,parsep=0pt]
\item We propose a world-model-based MARL framework for mixed-autonomy dynamic routing whose primary design objective is sample efficiency: the bulk of CAV policy updates are performed in learned latent traffic dynamics rather than in the microscopic simulator.
\item We design a graph-aligned action interface and traffic world model that lift executable per-CAV route choices onto segment-level latent transitions, combining graph convolutional traffic encoding with conditioned state-to-state flow matching and a history-conditioned, slow-timescale model of aggregate HDV demand.
\item We develop identity-preserving imagined route learning that couples a parameter-shared CAV actor with centralized per-vehicle and global critics, preserving vehicle-specific credit under a network-wide objective through a two-stage, simulator-grounded pipeline alternating online collection, world-model adaptation, and imagined actor-critic optimization.
\end{itemize}
Experiments on three urban road networks with 636, 1,035, and 2,068 scheduled vehicles, instantiated through the RouteRL and URB framework, show that UrbanDreamer reduces the real simulator interactions required to learn an effective routing policy by 69.8\%--77.3\% relative to the most sample-efficient MARL baseline on each network and lowers total wall-clock training time by 36\%--49\%, without compromising final routing performance.
